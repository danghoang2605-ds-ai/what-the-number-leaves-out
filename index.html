What the Number Leaves Out

I study data science and mathematics, and I often use numbers to explain my work. Here are three that need more explanation. Pick one to open its story, then tell me what you would ask next.

About 800 people

In high school, I helped organize a Vietnamese spoken-theater performance that drew nearly eight hundred people. I worked on staging, sound, and lighting. The audience count tells you how many people came. To explain my part, I need to tell you what I helped them see and hear.

That is what still interests me about theater. A decision about sound or lighting reaches someone sitting in the audience. Now, when I work on a technical project, I also want to know who will use it and what they need from it. I enjoy thinking about how something I help make reaches another person.

94% accuracy

At FPT Software, I worked on extracting information from medical records. One test produced a field-extraction accuracy score of 94 percent.

To understand that result, you need to know what counted as a correct extraction and which records we tested. I also need to explain a question I could not fully answer: how well did those records represent the larger collection?

The score describes a result for a particular task on a particular test. A broader claim about its usefulness would need more evidence. If I present a tool to someone else, I want to be able to explain that distinction before they depend on it.

49 years old

My mother is forty-nine and has joined a vocal class. She goes on weekends. Sometimes she sings for her high school students, who had known her as their mathematics teacher.

When I interviewed her, she gave three reasons for choosing mathematics instead of singing: earning a living, following her father's profession, and doubting whether she was good enough to sing professionally. I wanted to understand how those reasons fit together. I am still thinking about her answer.

She continues to teach mathematics, and now she makes time for singing too. Writing about her has made me realize how much a description such as “my mother is a teacher” can leave out. I want to remember to ask about the rest.

A note to future Dan

When you have a result to share, explain enough for someone to ask a useful question about it. Tell them what you did and what you still need to find out. Listen when they ask about a part you spent less time thinking about.

I hope you keep making things you are excited to show people. I hope there is still theater or music somewhere in your life. And when your mother tells you about her class, ask what she is singing.
